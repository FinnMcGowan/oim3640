Comparative Analysis of Economic Thought

Liberal economics, Marxism, and mercantilism through their major claims, goals, and foundational books.

Introduction

Liberal economics, Marxism, and mercantilism each offer a different explanation of how wealth is created, how power operates in an economy, and what role the state should play. Together, they form a useful comparison because each tradition defines prosperity and economic justice in a different way.

Liberal Economics

Liberal economics is most closely associated with Adam Smith's An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations (1776). Smith argued that markets, competition, and specialization can increase productivity and raise national wealth.

This tradition tends to trust voluntary exchange and price signals more than direct state control. The government still matters, but mainly to protect property, enforce contracts, and provide basic public goods.

Marxism

Marxism is grounded in Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels's The Communist Manifesto (1848) and Marx's Capital, Volume I (1867). Marx argued that capitalism produces enormous wealth but organizes that wealth through class ownership, allowing capitalists to profit from the labor of workers.

Unlike liberal economics, Marxism sees markets as conflict-ridden rather than self-correcting. It emphasizes exploitation, alienation, and the recurring instability of capitalist systems.

Mercantilism

Mercantilism was dominant in early modern Europe and is represented by works such as Thomas Mun's England's Treasure by Forraign Trade (1664). Mercantilists believed wealth and power were tied to the strength of the state, especially through trade surpluses, colonial control, and the accumulation of bullion.

This approach differs sharply from liberal economics because it favors active government intervention, tariffs, and trade restrictions when they increase national power.

Comparative Themes

Tradition Main Goal View of the State View of Markets
Liberal Economics Growth through exchange and productivity Limited but necessary Generally efficient and productive
Marxism Ending class exploitation Tool of class power, later restructured Structurally unequal and unstable
Mercantilism National power and trade advantage Central economic actor Useful only when directed by the state

Conclusion

Liberal economics centers the individual and the market, Marxism centers class and ownership, and mercantilism centers the state. Their differences are not just technical. They reflect competing beliefs about freedom, power, inequality, and national strength.
